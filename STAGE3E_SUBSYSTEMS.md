# Stage-3e — Multi-Subsystem Failure: Most of the Penalty Was an Artifact

**Model:** [`parameters_examples/haul_fleet_subsystems.json`](parameters_examples/haul_fleet_subsystems.json)
(generated by [`tools/`-style script](parameters_examples/haul_fleet_subsystems.json))
**Regression test:** [`engine/tests/haul_fleet_subsystems_v2.rs`](engine/tests/haul_fleet_subsystems_v2.rs)
**Critiques:** [`STAGE3D_SHOP_CAPACITY.md`](STAGE3D_SHOP_CAPACITY.md)

Stage-3d concluded that when the repair shop is the constraint, wear-levelling's synchronized
failures cost **67% more downtime** than naive. That model treated each truck as a **single
composite failure unit** with one load exponent — and that flattening is doing more of the work
than it should. A real truck fails by **independent subsystems with very different
load-sensitivities** (spec §1.1): tires (β ≈ 4), drivetrain (β ≈ 3), structure/engine (β ≈ 1.5).
Their failures are a *superposition of different-rate processes*, which aggregate wear-levelling
cannot synchronize. This stage tests how much of Stage-3d's penalty survives that correction.

## The model

Each truck now carries **three subsystems**, each its own `Fleet`-array with its own exponent
and life, resetting independently on overhaul. A truck is down if **any** subsystem is down
(`truck_available = avail_tires · avail_drive · avail_struct`, elementwise — which sidesteps the
engine's missing axis-selective reduction by keeping each mode as a separate array rather than a
`Fleet × Subsystem` matrix). [**Update 2026-07-29:** that reduction gap is now closed — see
`STAGE3F_AXIS_SELECTIVE_REDUCTION.md`; the per-mode-array workaround here is no longer forced, and
the `Fleet × Subsystem` reformulation is the follow-up.] Dispatch is unchanged — wear-levelling still argmins on the
**flattened** `total_damage_prev` (the composite health metric). The same fluid shop queue prices
the (now denser) failure stream.

## Result (300 realizations, seed fixed)

| Metric | Naive | Wear-levelling | Cohorting |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cumulative downtime | 94.9 | 123.5 | 121.5 |
| Peak repair backlog | 1.83 | 2.45 | 2.64 |
| **Wear-level ÷ naive downtime** | — | **1.30** | — |
| Per-mode failures (wear-level) | tires **20.3** · drive 12.0 · struct 4.0 | | |

### The penalty was ~55% artifact

The wear-levelling shop penalty drops from **1.67× (composite, Stage-3d) to 1.30×
(modal)** — so **most of the extra burstiness was an artifact of collapsing the truck's
subsystems into one failure event.** With modes split out, tires fail fast and often, structure
rarely, and the three clocks never line up, so the aggregate failure stream is far smoother than
a single-mode truck's. Aggregate wear-levelling can only synchronize the *composite* age; the
subsystems desynchronize underneath it.

**The effect is real but modest.** Wear-levelling still costs ~30% more downtime, for two
reasons that survive the correction: (1) it *does* still synchronize the composite, which
partially aligns the dominant (tires) mode; and (2) — the deeper point — it dispatches on the
**flattened** aggregate, blind to *which* subsystem is critical. Overloading hammers the
high-exponent tires (2.4× wear at 300 t) far more than the low-exponent structure (1.4×), so
"wear-level the truck total" can still be quietly overloading trucks whose tires are nearly gone.

### The composite model isn't wrong — it's a different question

Composite (Stage-3d) answers "how does dispatch interact with *whole-truck* replacement cycles";
modal (here) answers "how does it interact with the *actual* subsystem maintenance load." The
modal number (1.30×) is the more defensible one to quote to a maintenance manager, and it comes
with the standard RAM deliverable the composite can't give: **per-mode criticality** — tires
drive 56% of failures, drivetrain 33%, structure 11%.

## The sharper conclusion

The whole dispatch arc (3b→3e) lands here: the *ranking* of dispatch policies is robust
(cohorting for spread, staggering for shop-limited availability), but the **magnitude** of the
"balanced dispatch loses at the shop" effect is sensitive to failure-model fidelity, and the
single-composite model **overstates it by ~1.8×**. The right dispatch metric is almost certainly
**not** the flattened composite at all — it is subsystem-aware (protect the truck whose
*critical* mode is most worn, and condition overloading on *tire/frame* headroom, not total
damage). That is the next model, and it is also a cleaner statement of your critique: the flaw is
in the flattening, at both the failure layer *and* the dispatch layer.

## What this exercised

- A 3-mode × 6-truck failure model — 18 independent subsystem processes — built entirely from
  per-mode `Fleet` arrays + elementwise availability products, on today's engine. It also
  surfaced a real engine gap for the *general* multi-mode case: no **axis-selective reduction**
  over a `Fleet × Subsystem` matrix (worked around here by one array per mode; fine for 3 modes,
  awkward at 8). **This gap is now closed** — `STAGE3F_AXIS_SELECTIVE_REDUCTION.md`.
- Per-mode criticality ranking (tires > drivetrain > structure), a standard RAM output.
- Determinism: the wear-levelling run is bit-identical across repeats.

## Next

- **Subsystem-aware dispatch**: wear-level on the *most-critical* mode (`max` over
  `damage_prev_s`), or condition overloading on tire/frame headroom — then re-measure the shop
  penalty. Expect it to shrink further, and to expose whether "protect the critical mode" beats
  "balance the total."
- **Per-mode repair capacity**: tires and engines queue at *different* shops with different
  bay/mechanic counts — the point in your framing that the binding constraint is a specific
  resource (a tyre bay, a particular mechanic), not a generic one.
